A brain MRI looks like a picture. To a computer, it's a grid of numbers. So how do numbers become structures we can name... and measure?

This slice is synthetic: a phantom generated by code, not a patient. Upload it, and segmentation gives every pixel a label: one image, separated into layers. Scalp and skull. Gray matter. White matter. Fluid. And a simulated lesion.

Now take the lesion. Each pixel is a square, s millimeters wide, covering s squared. Count the pixels, N, multiply by s squared, and you have an area. The longest gap between two edge pixels gives its diameter.

That's the idea behind CortexView: generative where it helps, deterministic where it matters. In the design, an image model only decides how the illustration looks. The anatomy comes from the masks, and every number is computed from the pixels. Same input, same answer.

Here's where it stands. The phantom demo and its measurements run today, and a classical segmenter reads MRI series in Python. Fifty-three public ReMIND scans became training guides. Fine-tuning the style model is the next step.

One slice. Five layers. Numbers you can trace back to the pixels. CortexView: turning brain images into structured, measurable visual information. An educational aid, not a diagnosis.
